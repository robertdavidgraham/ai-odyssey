# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

## A PREFACE, CONTAINING THE MINIMUM YOU NEED TO KNOW

This poem is a sequel, and its poet never pretends otherwise. He assumes that you have heard all about the Trojan War, because everyone he sang for had; and he assumes you know one other story besides, a story about a homecoming, which he never tells straight through but circles all the way to the end of the book. I do not intend to load you up with mythology here — everything else will be explained as it comes, in the text or in the notes below the line — but these two things you should have in your pocket before Book One, because the poem leans on them from its opening page.

**First, the war.** A prince of Troy — a rich city across the sea, in what is now Turkey — was a guest in the house of Menelaus, king of Sparta, and left with his host's wife, Helen, the most beautiful woman in the world. Whether she went willingly is a question the Greeks themselves never settled, and neither will we. Menelaus's elder brother was Agamemnon, the most powerful king in Greece, and he raised an army from every Greek kingdom to fetch her back; among the kings who sailed with him, not especially willingly, was Odysseus of Ithaca, a small rocky island in the west. The siege of Troy lasted ten years, and the city fell in the end not to strength but to a trick — the great hollow horse of wood, with armed men in its belly — and the trick was Odysseus's. And then, in the hour of winning, the Greeks behaved so foully in the sacked city that the gods who had favoured them turned around; the homeward fleets were scattered, wrecked, and drowned, and hardly anyone got home clean. That is where our poem stands: the war has been over for ten years when it begins, and one man has still not come home.

**Second, the homecoming — the other one.** Agamemnon came home from Troy in triumph, the war won, the ships loaded. In his ten years away, his wife, Clytemnestra, had taken a lover: Aegisthus, a cousin of the king's from the bitter side of the family. The two of them welcomed Agamemnon home, sat him down to the feast of his return, and murdered him at the table. For years the deed stood unpunished, until Agamemnon's son, Orestes, grew up, came back, and killed his father's killers. Every character in the *Odyssey* knows this story; it is the recent news of their world, and it stands behind our poem like a shadow behind a lamp. A man is missing at sea; his wife waits at home surrounded by other men; his son is coming of age with a debt to settle. The poem never once lets you forget that this exact arrangement has just ended, in another royal house, in blood — and every wife, son, and homecoming in our story will be measured against that one, openly, by the characters themselves.

**The names you need, then, are six:**

- **Agamemnon** — high king of the Greeks, leader of the war, murdered on the day he came home.
- **Clytemnestra** — his wife, who did the murdering.
- **Aegisthus** — her lover, who did it with her.
- **Orestes** — their son, who avenged it.
- **Atreus** — Agamemnon's father, whose name hangs over the whole family; "son of Atreus" is how the poem usually says *Agamemnon*.
- **Menelaus** — Agamemnon's brother, Helen's husband, king of Sparta; he got home late, but he got home, and we will be visiting him.

That is the whole kit. Zeus, Athena, Poseidon and the rest you will meet as we go, and I will introduce them properly when they arrive. Turn the page; the gods are holding a meeting, and they are talking, as it happens, about Aegisthus.
